Examples of recent modifications

Click any item below to view the structure. A selection of modifications ordered at PPR Ltd — if the modification you require isn't listed here, get in touch, as these are only examples of what we can perform.

Acetylation & amidation →

Peptides can be acetylated at the N-terminus and amidated at the C-terminus at no extra cost. Both modifications mimic a peptide bond at the ends of the chain, increasing stability to peptidases.

Biotinylated peptides →

Biotin is usually coupled to a peptide via an amide bond, either at the N-terminus or a side-chain amino group. It's useful for affinity applications that rely on its very strong interaction with avidin/streptavidin.

Cyclisation →

Cyclic peptides can be produced by forming a disulfide bond between two cysteine residues, or by head-to-tail or side-chain cyclisation forming an amide bond. With suitable protecting groups, it's possible to cyclise between two specific cysteines, allowing more than one disulfide within a single peptide.

D-amino acids →

D-amino acids can be incorporated in place of the natural L-amino acids, either at a specific position or throughout the whole peptide — often to increase a peptide's stability to proteases.

Fluorescent labelled peptides →

A variety of fluorescent dyes — including fluorescein, rhodamine and cyanine dyes — can be attached to the N-terminus or a side-chain amino group. Fluorescence-quenched substrates help assess protease specificity and protein–protein interactions, and labelled peptides can be tracked within cells using specialist microscopy.

Phosphorylated serine, threonine and tyrosine →

Phosphorylated residues can be inserted in place of serine, threonine or tyrosine within a peptide sequence — commonly used in kinase and signalling research.

Peptide aldehydes →

A C-terminal aldehyde is a common feature of enzyme inhibitors, such as HIV protease or caspase inhibitors. Peptide aldehydes are also used in chemoselective ligation (for example, building peptide dendrimers) and in forming reduced, pseudo-peptide bonds.

N-methyl amino acids →

N-methylation replaces a backbone hydrogen-bond donor with a methyl group — useful for destabilising secondary structure and reducing peptide aggregation.

Thiols →

Cysteine residues can be oxidised to disulfides, producing cyclic peptides or peptide dimers. Thioester peptides can also be prepared for use in native chemical ligation.

Unnatural amino acids →

A wide variety of commercially available unnatural amino acids — for example butylglycine, pyroglutamic acid or norleucine — can create peptidomimetics with improved in-vivo half-life and potency. If an amino acid isn't available commercially, we may be able to synthesise it.

Urea bond peptides →

Urea bonds are often incorporated into peptide-based inhibitors — for example in β-secretase inhibitor design — as a stable alternative to a standard peptide bond.
